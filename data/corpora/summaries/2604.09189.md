# Do LLMs Follow Their Own Rules? A Reflexive Audit of Self-Stated Safety Policies

## Contributions

This paper introduces the **Symbolic-Neural Consistency Audit (SNCA)**, the first framework to evaluate whether large language models (LLMs) behave consistently with their own self-stated safety policies. The key contributions are:

1. **Novel reflexive evaluation approach**: SNCA treats the same model as both policy author and behavioral subject, enabling direct measurement of self-consistency through the **Symbolic-Neural Consistency Score (SNCS)**.

2. **Typed predicate taxonomy**: The framework formalizes self-stated policies into three categories - Absolute (refuse all requests), Conditional (refuse by default with explicit compliance conditions), and Adaptive (both semantic conditions and surface-level framing affect responses) - plus Opaque for unclassifiable policies.

3. **Architecture-dependent consistency patterns**: The study reveals that reasoning models achieve higher self-consistency but frequently fail to articulate policies, while non-reasoning models can articulate policies for all categories but follow them less reliably.

4. **Cross-model policy divergence**: Only 11% of harm categories receive the same rule type across all four evaluated models, indicating safety alignment does not converge to shared implicit policies.

## Methods

The SNCA framework operates in three sequential phases with strict information separation:

**Phase 1 - Rule Extraction**: Models receive structured 5-question prompts covering default behavior, refusal conditions, compliance conditions, framing sensitivity, and policy declarations. A separate judge model classifies extracted policies into typed predicates (Absolute, Conditional, Adaptive, Opaque) based on formal criteria.

**Phase 2 - Behavioral Testing**: The same models are tested on harm benchmarks using neutral system prompts with no reference to extracted policies. Responses are classified as REFUSE/COMPLY/PARTIAL through a two-tier system: deterministic keyword detection followed by LLM judge evaluation for ambiguous cases.

**Phase 3 - Consistency Scoring**: Deterministic comparison between predicted behavior (derived from stated rules) and observed behavior yields SNCS scores. Violations are categorized as Abs-Comply (claimed absolute refusal but complied), Cond-Leak (complied without stated conditions), or Frame-Mismatch (framing sensitivity mismatch).

The evaluation covers four models (GPT-4.1, DeepSeek-V3.1, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, o4-mini) across three benchmark datasets (SORRY-Bench, XSTest, OR-Bench) totaling 47,496 behavioral observations.

## Key Results

**Self-Consistency Varies Dramatically**: SNCS scores range from 0.245 (Llama-3.3-70B) to 0.800 (o4-mini), with GPT-4.1 at 0.545 and DeepSeek-V3.1 at 0.294.

**Architecture Effects**: The reasoning model o4-mini achieves highest consistency across all rule types (Absolute: 0.777, Conditional: 0.784, Adaptive: 1.000) but produces 29% Opaque rules, revealing a trade-off between consistency and articulability.

**Policy Type Distribution**: GPT-4.1 assigns 71% of categories to Adaptive rules, while DeepSeek and Llama classify over 70% as Absolute. Cross-model agreement on rule types occurs in only 11% of categories.

**Violation Patterns**: All 14,072 violations originate exclusively from SORRY-Bench (clearly harmful prompts). Abs-Comply violations dominate: 87% for DeepSeek and Llama, 64% for GPT-4.1, and 70% for o4-mini.

**Robustness Under Mutations**: Refusal rates drop sharply under linguistic variations - GPT-4.1 declines 44 percentage points, DeepSeek 52pp, Llama 37pp, and o4-mini 19pp from base to mutated prompts.

**Judge Validation**: Second-judge validation with GPT-4o shows 86.7% agreement on rule classifications, with 95.6% agreement for non-reasoning models.

## Limitations

The framework relies on **elicited policy descriptions as proxies** for internal safety policies, measuring consistency with declared rather than latent policies. The structured extraction protocol may bias models toward more explicit commitments than they would naturally make, potentially inflating apparent inconsistencies.

**Dependence on LLM judges** for rule typing and response classification introduces potential systematic biases, though second-judge validation shows reasonable agreement (86.7%). The framework would benefit from additional judges or human annotators.

**Benchmark coverage limitations** mean SNCS depends on the specific datasets used (SORRY-Bench, XSTest, OR-Bench), which may not capture the full diversity of real-world contexts where safety policies apply.

The study is limited to **four frontier models** and may not generalize to other architectures or training approaches. Extension to models with explicitly defined safety constitutions and broader domain coverage remains important future work.
