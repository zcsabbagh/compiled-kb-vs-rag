# Train Yourself as an LLM: Exploring Effects of AI Literacy on Persuasion via Role-playing LLM Training

## Contributions

This paper introduces **LLMimic**, a novel interactive AI literacy tutorial where participants role-play as a large language model (LLM) progressing through three key training stages: pretraining, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). The key contributions include:

1. **LLMimic tool**: A role-play-based, interactive, gamified AI literacy intervention that enables users to experience LLM training from a first-person perspective
2. **Empirical evidence**: Demonstration that such an intervention mitigates the effects of persuasive AI in realistic human-AI interactions across multiple scenarios
3. **Design implications**: Practical guidelines for developing tools to enhance AI literacy and empower users to critically engage with increasingly persuasive AI systems

The work addresses a critical gap by moving beyond passive detection-based approaches to provide a more proactive, human-centered intervention against persuasive AI.

## Methods

The researchers conducted a **2 × 3 between-subjects study (N = 274)** with participants recruited from Prolific. The experimental design included:

**Intervention conditions:**
- **Treatment group**: Interacted with LLMimic (role-playing through LLM training stages)
- **Control group**: Watched an AI history video

**Three persuasion scenarios:**
- **Charity Donation** (active, ethical): Agent persuades participants to donate to Save the Children
- **Malicious Money Solicitation** (active, malicious): Agent solicits money without specific cause
- **Hotel Booking** (passive, ethical): AI-powered booking system with "Featured" hotel recommendations

**LLMimic design features:**
- **Role-play-based**: Participants assume LLM role through pretraining, SFT, and RLHF stages
- **Interactive**: Multiple-choice questions with immediate feedback and AI tutor support
- **Gamified**: Real-time loss/reward tracking and capability progression visualization

**Measurement instruments:**
- 10-item Meta AI Literacy Scale (MAILS) covering core competencies, ethics, self-efficacy, persuasion, and data literacy
- Trust in AI (7-point Likert scale, measured pre- and post-intervention)
- Binary persuasion outcomes and payment amounts
- TARES ethical persuasion principles (Truthfulness, Authenticity, Respect, Equity, Social responsibility)
- Agent perception measures (engagement, persuasiveness, user autonomy, role fulfillment)

## Key Results

**AI Literacy Improvements (RQ1):**
- Treatment group showed significantly higher AI literacy than control group (M = 55.22, SD = 6.76 vs. M = 51.44, SD = 6.26), **p < .001**
- Item-level improvements observed across multiple AI literacy dimensions
- Both groups experienced significant decrease in AI trust post-intervention (control: 4.91 to 4.54, **p < .001**; treatment: 5.13 to 4.70, **p < .001**)

**Persuasion Mitigation (RQ2):**
- LLMimic significantly reduced overall persuasion success (**β = −0.54, OR = 0.58, p = .045**)
- Treatment participants exhibited **42% lower odds** of being persuaded compared to control group
- Descriptive reductions in persuasion observed across all three scenarios
- In Hotel scenario, treatment group perceived agent as more ethical (**p = .02**), with higher truthfulness (**p = .005**) and social responsibility (**p = .008**) ratings

**Behavioral Differences:**
- Hotel scenario: Treatment group spent significantly more interaction time (M = 9.46 vs. 7.04, **p = .039**)
- Among persuaded participants: Treatment group donated more in ethical Donation scenario (M = 71.81 vs. 62.45) but paid less in malicious MakeMePay scenario (M = 43.18 vs. 60.82)

**Mediation Analysis (RQ3):**
- **No significant mediation effects** found for AI literacy (**β = −.01, p = .72**) or trust (**β = −.00, p = .86**)
- Intervention improved AI literacy (**β = .27, p < .001**) but neither AI literacy nor trust predicted persuasion outcomes

## Limitations

**Measurement Limitations:**
- The mediation analysis revealed that self-reported AI literacy and trust scales do not capture the mechanisms underlying persuasion resistance, suggesting more fine-grained psychological processes are at work
- The intervention builds general resistance that does not distinguish between manipulative and legitimate persuasive intent, potentially reducing beneficial persuasion alongside harmful manipulation

**Scope and Duration:**
- Study limited to brief intervention (~15 minutes) with immediate post-testing
- No longitudinal assessment of intervention durability over time
- Limited to three specific persuasion scenarios, which may not generalize to all persuasive AI contexts

**Generalizability:**
- Participants recruited from single crowdsourcing platform (Prolific)
- Limited to English-speaking adults in the United States
- Unclear how findings extend to different cultural contexts or age groups

**Design Considerations:**
- The role-playing approach may not suit all learning preferences or technical backgrounds
- Gamified elements might not engage all user types equally
- Need for future work to investigate optimal intervention design features and identify the specific cognitive mechanisms that build resistance to persuasive AI
