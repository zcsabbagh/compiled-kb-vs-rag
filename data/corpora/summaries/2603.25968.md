# Neuro-Cognitive Reward Modeling for Human-Centered Autonomous Vehicle Control

## Contributions

This paper presents three main contributions to autonomous vehicle control:

- **Novel multimodal dataset**: The authors collected the first dataset combining EEG signals, eye-tracking data, behavioral data, and scene images from 20 participants using a realistic VR driving simulator. The dataset contains 720K frames across emergency braking and left turn scenarios.

- **Human cognitive reward model**: They developed a lightweight CNN that predicts event-related potential (ERP) strength directly from scene images, eliminating the need for real-time EEG data collection during inference while achieving 82% accuracy at 204 fps.

- **EEG-guided RLHF framework**: The authors propose integrating predicted cognitive load signals into reinforcement learning reward functions, demonstrating improved collision avoidance performance in complex driving scenarios.

## Methods

The methodology consists of four main components:

**Data Collection**: 32 participants were recruited (20 included in final analysis) to drive in VR using HTC Vive Pro Eye headset and CARLA simulator. EEG was recorded using 64-channel Synamps2 system at 1000 Hz. Two scenarios were designed: emergency braking (following a lead vehicle that brakes unexpectedly at 4-7 second intervals) and left turn (navigating intersections with oncoming traffic at 3-5 m/s).

**EEG Processing**: Raw EEG data underwent bandpass filtering (0.5-100 Hz), downsampling to 250 Hz, artifact removal, and ICA decomposition. ERPs were extracted using front vehicle braking as event markers, with trials classified as high-ERP or low-ERP using a peak-to-peak threshold of 1.7 µV.

**Cognitive Reward Model**: A lightweight 3-layer CNN predicts ERP presence from semantic segmentation images using Binary Cross-Entropy loss. The model processes sequences of three consecutive frames and outputs binary classification of ERP-inducing trials.

**RL Framework**: The problem is formulated as an MDP with state space comprising temporal sequences of semantic segmentation frames, continuous action space [-1,1] for throttle/brake control, and composite reward function: rt = βrcog(st) + rcollide + ωridle + δrgap, where β = -1 penalizes high cognitive load states. The policy network uses CNN feature extraction followed by self-attention and dual MLP heads predicting actions and time-to-collision.

## Key Results

**EEG Analysis**: Significant positive correlation found between ERP peak latency and reaction time (p = 0.0438). ERP amplitude was substantially higher during active response conditions compared to baseline, with significant differences between 300-500 ms confirmed by permutation testing with 10,000 iterations.

**Model Performance**: The cognitive reward model achieved 82% average accuracy across five-fold cross-validation, outperforming ResNet-18 (81%), Swin-ViT (79%), and ConvNeXt (80%). Processing speed reached 204 fps, saving 2.1 hours of training time compared to ResNet-18 when training RL for 1M steps.

**Driving Performance**: The EEG-guided RL framework demonstrated improved collision avoidance ability compared to vanilla TD3, Behavior Cloning, Prioritized Human In-the-Loop RL, TD3-lag, and traditional RLHF baselines across both emergency braking and left turn scenarios.

## Limitations

Several limitations constrain the scope and applicability of this work:

**Dataset Scale**: Only 20 participants were included in the final analysis (12 excluded due to VR motion sickness or insufficient engagement), limiting generalizability across diverse populations and driving behaviors.

**Scenario Specificity**: Testing was limited to two specific driving scenarios (emergency braking and left turn), raising questions about generalization to the full spectrum of autonomous driving situations.

**EEG Signal Constraints**: The approach relies on ERP detection with a fixed threshold (1.7 µV) that may not generalize across individuals with different baseline EEG characteristics or neurological variations.

**Simulation Environment**: All data collection occurred in VR simulation rather than real-world driving conditions, potentially limiting ecological validity and real-world applicability.

**Temporal Resolution**: The method depends on ERP responses occurring 300-500 ms after stimulus onset, which may introduce latency in real-time decision-making scenarios requiring immediate responses.

**Individual Differences**: The framework does not account for individual variations in cognitive processing, EEG signal characteristics, or driving expertise that could affect model performance across different users.
